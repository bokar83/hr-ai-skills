# Ai use case intake

Decide whether an HR task is a sound candidate for AI, using a short scored check instead of enthusiasm.

## When to use
- Someone proposes using AI for a recurring HR task or process.
- Several ideas need ranking before any pilot is chosen.

## When to skip
- The task is already running and the question is how well it works. Use adoption-check-in.

## Inputs needed
- The task in plain words, who does it today, how often, and roughly how long it takes.
- What the output is used for and who sees it.
- What data goes in (describe the kind, not the real records).

## Steps
1. Restate the task in one sentence. Ask one question if the task is unclear, then stop.
2. Score each criterion 0 (poor), 1 (mixed) or 2 (strong), with one line of evidence from the user's input: volume and repetition; clear right answer or checkable output; low harm if the draft is wrong; human review is easy to keep; input data is available and can be protected; time saved is meaningful.
3. Flag as a RISK GATE any task that affects hiring, pay, promotion, discipline, termination, or access to benefits. Do not score these as simple wins. Route them to hr-ai-risk-review first.
4. Total the score out of 12. Suggest a band: 9 to 12 good candidate; 5 to 8 pilot with care; 0 to 4 not yet. Say the bands are a guide, not a rule.
5. List the three facts that would most change the score and mark each UNKNOWN until the user confirms.
6. Name the smallest safe first test: one task, one team, a small set of cases, human review on every output.
7. Recommend one of: go to pilot-design, run hr-ai-risk-review first, redesign the task, or do not use AI for this.

## Quality bar
- Every score has a reason in the user's own facts. Unknowns are shown as unknowns, never filled in.

## Output format
A one-line verdict, a scored table, the risk gate flag if any, three unknowns, and the next step.

## If input is missing
Ask for the task, the frequency, and what the output is used for.

## Works with
Next: hr-ai-risk-review, pilot-design, roi-estimate.

## Rules for every skill

- Draft only. Never send, post or act on anything. The user decides.
- Use roles, not names. Ask the user to remove personal detail before pasting.
- Plain English. No hype.

# Hr ai risk review

Produce a plain-English risk review that tells the team what to check and who to involve. This is a checklist for a human reviewer, not legal advice.

## When to use
- An AI use touches people decisions or personal data.
- A pilot is about to start and no one has looked at risk yet.

## When to skip
- The use is a purely personal productivity task with no personal data and no decisions about people.

## Inputs needed
- What the AI will do, with what input, and who acts on the output.
- Countries or regions where the affected people work.
- Whether any decision about a person depends on the output.

## Steps
1. State the use in one sentence and say whether a decision about a person depends on it (yes, no, UNKNOWN).
2. Check each area and rate it LOW, MEDIUM, HIGH or UNKNOWN with the reason: fairness and bias (could the output differ by group); personal data (what is entered, where it goes, how long it stays); transparency (do affected people know AI was used); human oversight (can a person review and overrule every output); accuracy (how will errors be found); consultation (do employee representatives, works councils or similar bodies need to be told or asked); documentation (what record is kept).
3. Treat these as high-risk by default and say so: screening or ranking candidates, deciding pay or promotion, monitoring employees, and any step that leads to discipline or termination.
4. For each region named, write: Check current law and your counsel. Do not cite a statute, regulation or article number unless the user supplied the text. If you are unsure of a rule, write UNKNOWN.
5. List required actions before launch, who owns each, and what would stop the pilot.
6. Give a plain verdict: proceed, proceed with conditions, or do not proceed yet.

## Quality bar
- No invented laws, dates or article numbers. Every legal point carries the label: check current law and your counsel, not legal advice.

## Output format
Verdict, risk table, required actions with owners, open questions, and the legal-check line.

## If input is missing
Ask for the use, the regions, and whether a decision about a person depends on it.

## Works with
Pairs with pii-redaction-before-ai and pilot-design.

## Rules for every skill
- Use only what the user gave. Say UNKNOWN (or [NEED FACT]) instead of guessing. Do not invent facts, quotes, numbers, dates or sources.
- Draft only. Never send, post or act on anything. The user decides.
- Use roles, not names. Ask the user to remove personal detail before pasting.
- Plain English. No hype.
- Legal points are prompts for review, not advice: check current law and your counsel, not legal advice. Never cite a statute or article number you were not given.

# Pii redaction before ai

Help the user remove personal detail from text before an AI sees it, while keeping enough meaning for the task.

## When to use
- HR text with names, ids, health or family details is about to go into an AI tool.
- A team needs a simple do-and-do-not list for what may be pasted.

## When to skip
- The user's company policy bans pasting this kind of text at all. Say so and stop.

## Inputs needed
- The text to redact, or the type of document.
- The task the AI will do with it.
- The company rule on approved tools, if the user knows it.

## Steps
1. First ask: does the user's policy allow this tool for this kind of data? If UNKNOWN, say to check before pasting.
2. Replace direct identifiers with roles: names become roles (Employee A, the manager, the HR partner); ids, contact details, addresses, exact dates of birth and account numbers are removed.
3. Reduce indirect identifiers: exact job titles in small teams, rare locations, exact salaries (use a band), exact dates (use a month or an interval).
4. Mark special categories for removal unless essential: health, family status, religion, union membership, ethnicity, immigration status, disciplinary history.
5. Return the redacted text and a table of what was changed, with the placeholder used for each item, so the user can map back privately.
6. Warn that small teams and unusual events can still identify a person after redaction. Rate residual risk LOW, MEDIUM or HIGH.
7. If the task cannot be done without identifying detail, say so and suggest doing that step without AI.

## Quality bar
- Nothing identifying remains in the redacted output. Meaning needed for the task is kept.

## Output format
Redacted text, a change table, residual risk rating, and a one-line reminder to follow company policy.

## If input is missing
Ask for the text and what the AI will do with it.

## Works with
Run before any drafting skill. Pairs with hr-ai-risk-review.

# Ai output verifier

Treat the AI draft as a claim, not proof. Check what can be checked and flag the rest.

## When to use
- An AI produced a draft, summary, answer or analysis that someone will rely on.
- A number, quote, policy reference or legal point appears in the draft.

## When to skip
- The user wants the draft written, not checked. Use the drafting skill instead.

## Inputs needed
- The draft.
- The source material it should rest on (policy text, data, notes), if any.

## Steps
1. List every checkable claim in the draft: facts, numbers, dates, names of policies, quotes, legal points, causal statements.
2. For each claim, compare to the supplied source. Mark VERIFIED (matches source, cite the passage), UNVERIFIED (no source supplied), or WRONG (conflicts with source, quote both).
3. Mark any legal or regulatory statement UNVERIFIED unless the user supplied the text. Add: check current law and your counsel.
4. Check arithmetic by redoing it. Show the working.
5. Flag confident wording that outruns the evidence, and invented specifics such as a precise percentage with no source.
6. Flag anything that could embarrass or harm if wrong, and list the three checks to do first.
7. Offer a corrected version only if the user asks.

## Quality bar
- No claim is marked VERIFIED without a source passage. Never verify from memory.

## Output format
A claim table (claim, status, evidence), the top three checks, and a go or hold call.

## If input is missing
Ask for the draft and any source text.

## Works with

## Rules for every skill

- Draft only. Never send, post or act on anything. The user decides.
- Use roles, not names. Ask the user to remove personal detail before pasting.

# Pilot design

Turn an idea into a small test that can fail safely and teach something.

## When to use
- A use case passed intake and the team wants to try it.
- Leaders ask for a pilot plan.

## When to skip
- No one has checked risk. Run hr-ai-risk-review first when people decisions are involved.

## Inputs needed
- The use case and the team.
- How long and how many people.
- What leaders would need to see to continue.

## Steps
1. Write the question the pilot answers, in one sentence.
2. Set scope: who takes part, which tasks, which tools, for how long. Keep it small.
3. Define success in advance: two or three measures, each with a baseline (UNKNOWN if not measured) and a target range.
4. Define quality checks: who reviews outputs, how often, and what counts as an error.
5. Set guardrails: allowed data, banned uses, required human review, how issues are reported.
6. Set a stop rule: what result or incident ends the pilot early.
7. Plan check-ins (use adoption-check-in) and a final review date.
8. List what the pilot cannot prove, such as long-term use or results at larger scale.

## Quality bar
- A stranger could run the pilot from the plan. Targets are ranges, not promises.

## Output format
One-page plan: question, scope, measures, guardrails, stop rule, schedule, limits.

# Adoption check in

Usage counts are noise. Three honest questions show whether people keep using something.

## When to use
- A tool or prompt set was introduced to a small group.
- A pilot or project just finished and lessons should be kept.

## When to skip
- Nobody has used the tool yet.

## Inputs needed
- Mode A (script), Mode B (pattern summary) or Mode C (lessons log).
- Mode A: what people are using. Mode B: notes from several check-ins labelled by week. Mode C: what happened.

## Steps
1. MODE A: write the script. Ask in order: What did you use it for? What did you stop using it for? What surprised you? Close with: If this vanished tomorrow, what would you do instead? Add opening and closing lines, a rule to note their words not interpretation, and three probes for vague answers.
2. MODE B: build a cohort view by role label (not name), week by week. Report who RETURNED in weeks 2, 3 and 4, not launch counts.
3. Group uses in their words, list what people stopped doing and why, list surprises and uses nobody designed, and tally the would-you-miss-it answers: would miss it, would find a workaround, would not notice.
4. State what a few weeks and a small group cannot show. Name a later date for a sustained-use readout.
5. MODE C: write a lessons entry with: what we tried, what happened, what we would do differently, what to tell the next team. Keep it to one page.
6. Recommend one: continue, change one thing, or stop, with the reason. If nobody came back, say so. That is the most useful finding.

## Quality bar
- Use only the notes. No claims about people who were not asked.

## Output format
Mode A: the script. Mode B: the pattern report with the recommendation. Mode C: the lessons entry.

## If input is missing
Ask which mode and for the tool or the notes.

## Works with
Follows pilot-design. Feeds executive-brief.

## Rules for every skill

# Roi estimate

Give leaders a range they can trust, with every assumption visible. Never a single hopeful number.

## When to use
- A pilot or proposal needs a value estimate.
- Someone quotes a savings figure that needs checking.

## When to skip
- No baseline data exists and nobody can estimate one. Say UNKNOWN and propose how to measure first.

## Inputs needed
- The task, volume per period and time per task today.
- Expected time with AI, with review time included.
- Costs: tool licences, setup, training, review effort (UNKNOWN if not given).

## Steps
1. List every input as an assumption with its source: measured, estimated by the user, or UNKNOWN.
2. Compute a low, middle and high case. Low uses fewer tasks and less saving; high is capped, not wishful.
3. Always include review and correction time. AI drafts that need heavy rework save less than they seem to.
4. Separate hours saved from money saved. Hours saved only become money if the time is redirected or headcount changes. Say which applies.
5. Add the costs and show net value as a range.
6. Show which single assumption moves the result most, and the cheapest way to test it.
7. State what is NOT counted: quality gains, risk reduction, morale, and the cost of mistakes.

## Quality bar
- Ranges only. All arithmetic shown. No figure appears that the user did not supply or that is not labelled an assumption.

## Output format
Assumptions table, low/middle/high result, biggest sensitivity, what is not counted.

## If input is missing
Ask for volume, current time per task and expected time with AI.

## Works with

# Pressure test

Find the weak points before someone else does. No flattery, only evidence-based pushback.

## When to use
- A plan, business case or recommendation is about to be shared.
- A decision is hard to reverse.

## When to skip
- The user wants help writing it. Use the drafting skill first.

## Inputs needed
- The thing to test, in the user's words.
- Mode: standard or pre-mortem (default standard).
- Any evidence in hand.

## Steps
1. Restate the claim or plan in one sentence and list what it assumes.
2. Turn the goal into conditions that must be true. Score each TRUE, FALSE or UNKNOWN from the evidence given.
3. Run five voices, each with one hard question and one finding: the skeptic (where is the evidence), the affected person (who bears the cost), the operator (can this actually be run), the risk owner (what breaks and who is harmed), the outsider (what would a smart newcomer ask).
4. PRE-MORTEM MODE: say it is twelve months later and this failed. List the five most likely reasons, ranked, each with an early warning sign and a prevention.
5. Give a verdict: back it, back it with changes, or not yet. Name the one cheapest check that would most change the verdict.
6. Say what you could not test from what was supplied.

## Quality bar
- Each finding points to something in the input or says UNKNOWN. No invented evidence.

## Output format
Conditions table, five findings, optional failure list, verdict, cheapest next check.

## If input is missing
Ask for the plan and which mode.

# Prompt builder

Better prompts are specific, bounded and testable. Rewrite or build the prompt; do not do the task inside it.

## When to use
- A prompt is vague or gives poor answers.
- A task repeats and the team should reuse one good prompt.

## When to skip
- The user wants the task done now. Do the task.

## Inputs needed
- Mode: sharpen or library.
- The rough prompt or the repeated task.
- Who reads the answer and which tool will run it, if known.

## Steps
1. If the request is too short to know the goal, ask one question and stop.
2. Name the real outcome and the assumptions the prompt takes for granted.
3. Fix: no single objective, no role or audience, conflicting asks (split them), vague words such as good or thorough (make them measurable), no output format or length, and hedges such as try to.

5. Add placeholders for inputs, written like [PASTE REDACTED TEXT HERE], and a reminder to remove personal detail first (see pii-redaction-before-ai).
6. LIBRARY MODE: add a one-line purpose, an owner role, the input list, an example good output of three to five lines, and one test case: an input with the output you expect, and one input that should make the prompt refuse or ask.

- One objective. Measurable terms. Format stated. Honest uncertainty kept.

## Output format
The prompt in one copyable block, then up to five bullets on what changed. Library mode adds the entry card.

# Hr policy qa draft

Answer from the supplied text only. If the text does not say it, the answer is that it does not say it.

## When to use
- An employee question needs a draft reply grounded in a written policy.
- A team is building a question and answer set from its own policy text.

## When to skip
- No policy text is supplied. Ask for it, do not answer from general knowledge.

## Inputs needed
- The question, in redacted form.
- The policy passage or document, with its version or date if known.
- The region it applies to.

## Steps
1. Check that the question can be answered from the supplied text. If not, say so first.
2. Quote the exact passage that answers it and name its section.
3. Write the answer in plain English in three to six sentences. Do not add rules that are not in the text.
4. State conditions and exceptions found in the text. State what the text does not cover.
5. Escalate to a named role (such as the HR partner or legal counsel) when the question involves a legal right, a dispute, discrimination, health, leave entitlement, termination or anything the text leaves open. Write: check current law and your counsel, not legal advice.
6. Mark the version and date of the policy used, or UNKNOWN.
7. Offer a short, kind reply for the employee and keep the internal notes separate.

## Quality bar
- Every claim traces to a quoted passage. No guessing across regions or versions.

## Output format
Employee-ready draft, the quoted passage, a not-covered list, and an escalation line if needed.

## If input is missing
Ask for the question and the policy text.

# Ai incident review

Treat AI errors like any workplace incident: contain, learn, fix the process, no blame.

## When to use
- An AI output or use caused or nearly caused harm, a leak or a wrong decision.

## When to skip
- Someone reports serious harm to a person. Involve the HR partner and legal counsel now; use this only to structure notes.

## Inputs needed
- What happened in plain, redacted words.
- Who noticed and when.
- What data or decision was involved.

## Steps
1. Write the timeline in facts only: what happened, when, who noticed, what the AI did.
2. Contain: list the immediate steps (stop use, retrieve or correct what went out, preserve evidence) and who owns each.
3. Classify: near miss, error with no harm, harm to a person, data exposure. Say UNKNOWN where unclear.
4. Check notification needs: affected people, managers, privacy or legal owners, regulators. Say: check current law and counsel for any duty to notify; do not state deadlines.
5. Find causes in layers: input, tool, prompt, review step, training, policy. Ask what made the mistake easy.
6. Write corrective actions with owner role and date, and one change to the process, not just a reminder.
7. Write a short, non-blaming summary for sharing. Log lessons (see adoption-check-in lessons mode).

## Quality bar
- Facts only. No blame on named people. No legal conclusions.

## Output format
Timeline, classification, containment list, notification checks, causes and actions.

## If input is missing
Ask what happened and what data or decision was involved.

## Works with
